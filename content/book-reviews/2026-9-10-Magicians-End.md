Raymond E. Feist

&#9733; &#9733; &#9733; &#9733; &#9734;

As the culmination of a writing career spanning 30 years, this book does a wonderful job of wrapping up loose ends, and finishing the story. The matter of "finishing" any epic fantasy is always a difficult one, especially when the world has grown so large that a great many parts must necissarily be left out. But _Magician's End_ does manage to complete the Riftwar Cycle in the most satisfying way; the way a good stew and hearty bread fill you up so you know you've had a good meal. The immediate comparison for me is to _The Wheel of Time_, and while I believe WoT had the _correct_ ending that it needed, it was not nearly as satisfying for the reader as the ending of _Magician's End_. While WoT ends in a perfect neural way, one of my favorite facts about Feist's writing shines through again; his ending is _hopeful_.

My one negative about this book is that it spends a huge amount of time with a hero defending a siege. That's just boring. I want the action scenes of repelling seigers early or with a surprise counterattack. Simply outlasting a siege is not all that interesting in terms of medieval warfare.

Feist once again demonstrates that he is a master of the recap; effective without being obvious or overbearing. You could read this book as your first in the entire Riftwar, or as the last. Feist's books are above all, entertaining. They are easy to read, fun, and interesting. They are an impressive legacy.

There were a few scenes that were absolutely amazing in this book:

- Visiting the spectres from the past is very Neil Gaiman-like in the good ways. So is the very end of the book when Pug is inside the Void and exploring Time.
- Interactions with the gods of Midkemia, and Pug's realization: "You interact with humans because... We made you!"
- And possibly one of the most badass-because-it's-understated scenes in literature:

> _I understand_, said Pug. And he entered time.

TIME itself! A sorcerer so powerful that with _knowledge_ he can will himself to bend time to his will and view it as a tool to be used.

I also find it interesting when you can see where different inspirations have come from, for example:

- Valheru armor seems inspired by Tolkien's rings. The wearer becomes the object as the object imparts it's power.
- Magic is pretty much shown to be string theory and the energy spectrum... Magic is the ability to manipulate the physics modern man understands, but with just the mind.
- The Wheel of Time of course, in particular at the very last page.

Especially at the last 100 pages or so, this book would work really really well at a movie. The pacing and skipping around actually works really well to wrap up the story for several different heroes.

Theorem: all fantasy must boil down to Light vs Dark because that is how we play with the ultimate question. Are we alone? What else is there? What is out there which cannot be seen? Light defines what _is_.
