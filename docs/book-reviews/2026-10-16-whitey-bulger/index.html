Whitey Bulger

October 16, 2026

Kevin Cullen and Shelley Murphy

★ ★ ☆ ☆ ☆

This book’s primary downfall is that it suffers from the Historical Facts Problem. What I wanted out of this book was to learn from a heavily flawed figure: what mental toughness helped him survive Alcatraz, how did growing up in the Southie projects shape his outlook on work, crime, getting help from on high, snitching, maybe reconciling snitching with “informing”… Without all of these, Whitey is just an opportunistic grifter, who maybe by sheer statistics, happens to be the popular and successful one. The author’s efforts to be neutral and not pass judgement are so successful that the book is overall a fail because there is no opinion, just a bland textbook.

I wanted to find a hero in Whitey. A flawed one of course, with demons to fight and enemies in high places; the underdog people’s hero - the local mythic mobster from Boston. I wanted to learn how to become a leader and build up an isolated and downtrodden community.

Instead I found a self-absorbed loser who took advantage of a broken system. Whitey’s life was the game of cops and robbers we played as kids; he suffered near zero consequences for almost all his actions. It’s hard to find the right insult for Whitey. He’s much worse than just a dirtbag, but not quite a terrorist or monster. He’s just a selfish loser who took advantage of a happenstance place in the system where he could exploit it without consequences.

Bob said that Whitey was very intelligent, and that he could have been anything he wanted to be, but apparently he wanted to be a criminal.

I can’t quite call any of Whitey’s life a tragedy because he chose to be as awful as he did, but his life appeared to start with some Robinhood moments. When bussing was pulling his community apart and those forcing it’s implementation weren’t the ones living with the effects, “Bus Teddy” was a pretty awesome message. And in his diary later, connecting this to Ted Kennedy’s killing of Mary Jo Kopechne on Chappaquiddick Island is a good demonstration of the few ways to get politicians to face consequences of their actions. Unfortunately, this eventually exactly mirrored Whitey’s own lack of consequences due to brothers in high places.

At first I thought this was a pretty fun book to read, until you realize that the absolutely mockery of justice from the FBI is pretty much how the justice system in America has always been and still continues to function. In my opinion, laws derive their power from being a proxy for morality, and laws are not moral unless they are applied equally. Since they aren’t, we are free to ignore any law we would like. Those in power sure do.

Wrongful death cases against the Justice Department and FBI were mostly thrown out because statute of limitations had expired. But the government is not a single person, it is an organization that allowed its own cancer to exist and spread, linked by role not individual. Therefore I belive an organization or government cannot morally have a statute of limitations. Maybe democracy didn’t fail as recently as I thought.

Bill Bulger might be an even worse criminal that Whitey. He didn’t just try to support his brother; he enabled Whitey and shares responsibility for Whitey’s murders. And Bill was of course supported by Ted Kennedy. We’ve also got him to thank for ruining the MBTA, or “Mister Bulger’s Transit Authority”.

At his capture, Whitey acted like he was the smartest person in the room. This is always a fatal mistake. For every skill you have, there are professionals who do it dozens of times per day and are so good at it they are comfortable exercising those skills. They have put in the reps and will always beat you.

At the end of his trial Whitey said “They call it security, I call it torture… Designed to break me down”.

Good.
